CMS Redesign & Design System - Chronicle.com

Designing and improving digital products for higher education journalism and data hub The Chronicle of Higher Education

Role:
Lead Product Designer

Timeline:
August 2023 - September 2024

Team:
Myself, 3 Engineers, Product Manager

Tools:
Figma, Figjam, LESS/CSS, Adobe Analytics

The Challenge

Chronicle.com's CMS had evolved organically over years, accumulating significant design and technical debt. Editorial teams were frustrated with inconsistent components, limited functionality, and a dated visual appearance that no longer reflected the publication's authority in higher education journalism.

Key problems we identified:

  • Inconsistent user experience: Components had inconsistent UI and accessibility issues, confusing readers and diluting brand trust
  • Editorial team friction: Editorial and visuals staff had to work around CMS limitations and wished for more flexibility in how they told stories
  • Mounting technical debt: Lots of one-off CSS overrides, inconsistent variables and properties applied across the site, along with improperly built modules
  • Visual stagnation: The site's appearance hadn't evolved with modern web standards, impacting perceived credibility

The business need was clear: modernize the platform without disrupting daily publishing operations across a newsroom serving thousands of higher education professionals.

Research & Discovery

Design System Audit

I started by leading a comprehensive audit of our existing design system and CMS components, cataloging:

  • 50+ CMS and marketing modules with varying levels of usage and overlap
  • Inconsistent typography implementations (including incorrect fonts and >25 different font sizes)
  • Color values hard-coded in 30+ locations, many of which were incorrect shades
  • Components built without accessibility considerations

Proto-Design System Review

I catalogued all of our design tokens and components and created a proto-design system to work off of, which I pared down with the help of the visuals team.

In this process, we identified and eliminated 20+ redundant or unnecessary design tokens and variables, and assigned the remaining to variables in the CSS.

Design Strategy

Working with the product team, we planned a systematic, module-by-module migration that would allow editorial teams to continue publishing without interruption and enable thorough testing of each component before widespread deployment.

Foundation First Approach

Working closely with engineering, I established the groundwork by creating a design system that restructured color, typography, spacing, and other foundational styles. This was documented in Figma and implemented as reusable variables in LESS. I created clear naming conventions that bridged designer and developer language and built flexibility for future theme variations.

After variables were established, we moved on to modules. These fell into three groups:

  • Existing modules that only needed a light touch and QA review to ensure they were consistent with our new variables
  • Existing modules that needed redesign and a visual update
  • Existing modules that needed to be rebuilt almost completely
  • New modules to the base theme that we needed to design and adapt for our uses

The Plot Twist: Mid-Project Rebrand

Halfway through the migration, The Chronicle introduced new brand guidelines. This could have derailed the project, but our systematic approach proved its value.

I quickly audited which completed work needed revision, worked with the engineering team to restructure our LESS variables to accommodate the new brand system, re-prioritized the component queue based on brand visibility, and led design workshops with stakeholders and our UX Director to align on how the new brand should translate to digital.

The early investment in a proper design system meant we could propagate brand changes across dozens of components in a fraction of the time the old system would have required.

This validated our technical approach and became proof-of-concept for leadership that the redesign was worth the investment.

Design QA Standards

To ensure consistency across the redesign, I developed comprehensive Design QA documentation used company-wide. This guide covered color usage and accessibility requirements, typography specifications and hierarchy rules, spacing and layout grid systems, component states and interactive behavior, and responsive breakpoint standards.

This greatly minimized the design and visual errors across the company's digital products, and gave back hours of time weekly to the UX team.

Implementation & Training

Phased Rollout

I worked with engineering to migrate and test components in strategic phases:

  1. Homepage modules (highest visibility, user testing ground)
  2. Global elements (header, footer, etc.)
  3. Article templates (core reading experience)
  4. Section pages (editorial flexibility)
  5. Specialized tools (data visualizations, multimedia, visual tools)

Cross-Functional Training

I led and assisted with multiple training sessions across departments, showing editorial teams new CMS capabilities and layout options, training marketing folks on branding applications and accessibility practices, and walking others through the Design QA guide.

The Result

The redesigned Chronicle.com launched with a modern, cohesive visual system and a wider library of usable modules that better serves both our readers and editorial teams.

A before and after view of The Chronicle's homepage.

What changed:

  • Unified user experience with consistent typography, spacing, and component behavior across all page types
  • Expanded CMS with reusable and interactive modules that improved editorial's ability to tell stories
  • Flexible design system that enabled rapid implementation of new brand guidelines mid-project and brand consistency
  • Scalable foundation that reduced development time for new features and eliminated the technical and design debt that had accumulated over years

Qualitative feedback:
Editorial teams reported smoother publishing workflows and more layout flexibility. The development team noted faster implementation times for new features and updates. The design system and QA guide drastically decreased time needed for prototyping and design QA in the product team.

What I Learned

Start with the foundation. Investing time upfront in design tokens and system architecture paid massive dividends when requirements changed mid-project.

Incremental beats revolutionary. The phased approach maintained team morale and allowed us to course-correct based on real feedback as needed.

Documentation is design work. The design system and QA guide became as valuable as the visual designs themselves, enabling consistency at scale.

Collaboration requires translation. My role wasn't just designing components—it was bridging the language between editorial needs, business goals, and technical constraints to create a system that worked for everyone.
